Mixture Distributions and Bayesian Mixture Models

A mixture distribution is any probability distribution obtained by averaging a family of component distributions against a mixing measure \(Q\in\mathcal P(\Theta)\). Given a parametric family \(\{p_\theta(y\mid x):\theta\in\Theta\}\), we write

\[ p_Q(y\mid x)=\int_\Theta p_\theta(y\mid x)\,Q(d\theta). \]

When \(Q=\sum_{k=1}^K w_k\delta_{\theta_k}\), this becomes a finite mixture distribution,

\[ p_Q(y\mid x)=\sum_{k=1}^K w_kp_{\theta_k}(y\mid x), \qquad w_k\geq0,\quad\sum_{k=1}^K w_k=1. \]

When \(Q\) has infinitely many support points, the representation is an infinite mixture. The mixing measure may be discrete or continuous. These terms describe the mathematical representation of a probability distribution, without specifying how \(Q\) is estimated.

A mixture model, by contrast, is a statistical model that explicitly uses such a mixture representation to describe the data-generating distribution. A Bayesian mixture model additionally places priors on the unknown mixing weights and component parameters (Richardson and Green 1997). For instance, for \(K\) components, let \(\Pi\) be a prior distribution on the component parameter space \(\Theta\), and let \(\boldsymbol\alpha=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_K)\) contain positive concentration parameters. A common specification is

\[ \boldsymbol w\sim\operatorname{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol\alpha), \qquad \theta_k\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\Pi, \]

The component parameters \(\theta_k\) are independent draws from \(\Pi\), while the Dirichlet prior governs the nonnegative weights. Given observations \(\mathcal D\), Bayesian inference produces a joint posterior \(p(\boldsymbol w,\theta_{1:K}\mid\mathcal D)\) through the mixture likelihood. In contrast, Bayesian nonparametric mixtures may place a prior on the entire mixing measure, such as \(Q\sim\operatorname{DP}(\gamma,\Pi)\), where \(\gamma>0\) controls concentration and \(\Pi\) is the base distribution (Ferguson 1973). A Dirichlet-process draw is almost surely discrete, with countably many potential atoms; it is not generally a continuous mixing density.

Importantly, a mixture distribution is not necessarily the predictive distribution of a Bayesian mixture model. Even ordinary Bayesian model averaging produces a mixture distribution by integrating over a posterior, without requiring that the original likelihood be specified as a mixture model.

PrO as a Potentially Infinite Mixture

The predictively oriented posterior (PrO) (McLatchie et al. 2025) is a probability measure \(Q_n^\dagger\) on the original parameter space \(\Theta\), defined through the optimisation

\[ Q_n^\dagger = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{Q\in\mathcal P(\Theta)} \left\{ -\frac{\lambda_n}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n\log p_Q(y_i\mid x_i) + \mathrm{KL}(Q\Vert\Pi) \right\}, \]

where

\[ p_Q(y\mid x)=\int_\Theta p_\theta(y\mid x)\,Q(d\theta). \]

Consequently, the PrO posterior predictive is a mixture distribution, potentially involving infinitely many components. More precisely, PrO optimises over mixing measures rather than fixing the number of components in advance. For a non-atomic prior \(\Pi\), finite KL divergence requires \(Q\ll\Pi\), so the optimal mixing measure may be continuous.

However, PrO is not a Bayesian mixture model in the conventional generative sense. It does not specify a mixture likelihood with a prior over its unknown mixing measure. Instead, it constructs a distribution over the original model parameters by directly optimising predictive performance, subject to KL regularisation.

This is the distinction emphasised in Section 4.3 of McLatchie et al. (2025). The mathematical representation of the PrO predictive is a mixture, but its inferential construction and interpretation differ from conventional Bayesian mixture modelling.

Under the logarithmic score, PrO is also closely related to KL-regularised nonparametric maximum likelihood estimation of mixing distributions. Indeed, without the KL penalty, its empirical objective reduces to the classical nonparametric mixture likelihood problem studied by Kiefer and Wolfowitz (1956) and Lindsay (1983). The KL penalty is the additional regularisation in PrO; these classical references establish the unregularised mixture-likelihood connection, rather than the precise PrO regulariser.

Finite-Particle PrO and Tempered Bayesian Mixtures

The connection becomes more explicit in the finite-particle approximation developed in McLatchie et al. (2026).

Consider \(m\) particles \(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_m\) and their empirical measure

\[ \widehat Q_m=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m\delta_{\theta_j}. \]

For any fixed particle configuration, the associated predictive distribution is an equally weighted finite mixture,

\[ p_{\widehat Q_m}(y\mid x) = \frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m p_{\theta_j}(y\mid x). \]

The equal weights \(1/m\) belong to the empirical particle measure, not to a general PrO mixing measure. More precisely, if \(\widehat Q_m\Rightarrow Q\) and \(\theta\mapsto p_\theta(y\mid x)\) is bounded and continuous, then

\[ \frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m p_{\theta_j}(y\mid x)\longrightarrow\int_\Theta p_\theta(y\mid x)\,Q(d\theta). \]

Thus, uniformly weighted particles can approximate a highly non-uniform, even continuous, mixing measure through their spatial concentration. Unlike the Bayesian finite mixture above, the particle approximation fixes all component weights at \(1/m\); it does not assign them a Dirichlet prior.

The authors construct a Gibbs distribution over the joint particle space \(\Theta^m\):

\[ q_{n,m}(\theta_{1:m}) \propto \prod_{i=1}^n \left[ \frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m p_{\theta_j}(y_i\mid x_i) \right]^{m\lambda_n/n} \prod_{j=1}^m\pi(\theta_j). \]

This distribution is exactly a tempered Bayesian posterior for an equally weighted finite mixture model, with independent component priors and likelihood tempering parameter \(\alpha=m\lambda_n/n\). When \(\alpha=1\), it coincides with ordinary Bayesian inference under that mixture model.

It is important to distinguish three objects here: the random empirical mixing measure \(\widehat Q_m\), the joint Gibbs distribution \(Q_{n,m}\) over particle configurations, and its one-particle marginal

\[ \overline Q_{n,m} = \mathbb E_{Q_{n,m}}[\widehat Q_m]. \]

The latter approximates the original PrO posterior. Under the conditions in McLatchie et al. (2026),

\[ \mathrm{KL}(\overline Q_{n,m}\Vert Q_n^\dagger) \lesssim\frac{\lambda_n}{m}. \]

Thus, finite-particle PrO admits an exact interpretation through tempered Bayesian inference under a finite mixture model, while its one-particle marginal converges to the original PrO posterior as \(m\to\infty\).

This does not mean that PrO is simply an ordinary Bayesian infinite mixture model. In particular, the tempering parameter increases with \(m\) when \(n\) and \(\lambda_n\) are fixed.

Mixtures of Experts and PrO

A mixture of experts (MoE) (Jacobs et al. 1991; Jordan and Jacobs 1994) is a conditional mixture model in which the mixing weights depend on the input \(x\). A finite MoE has the form

\[ p(y\mid x) = \sum_{k=1}^K w_k(x)p_{\theta_k}(y\mid x), \]

where \(w_k(x)\geq0\) and \(\sum_{k=1}^K w_k(x)=1\). The functions \(w_k(x)\) are called gating functions, and allow different components to specialise in different regions of the input space.

The important distinction is between global mixing and input-dependent mixing. In a conventional conditional mixture with fixed weights, the same mixing measure is used for every \(x\), even though the component densities themselves depend on \(x\). In an MoE, the mixing measure varies with \(x\).

Standard PrO uses a globally shared mixing measure,

\[ p_Q(y\mid x) = \int_\Theta p_\theta(y\mid x)\,Q(d\theta), \]

and is therefore not an MoE in the usual sense.
